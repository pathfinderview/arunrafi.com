Eurekas

How a mechanical watch works

A second, taken apart

A mechanical watch has no battery and no chip. It has a coiled spring, a few wheels, and a small ring that swings back and forth eight times a second. This page takes it apart one idea at a time. Each chapter asks a question, gives you something to try, and ends in a small eureka when you work out the answer yourself.

Set to your time. The ring swinging in the window at nine o'clock is the balance wheel. By the end you will know exactly what it is doing, and why the whole watch obeys it.

The big question

How does a spring become a second?

Every clock ever built does two jobs. It stores energy, and it spends that energy in exactly equal pieces. In a watch, a spring stores it. Gears divide it. A stop-and-go lever lets it out a step at a time, and a swinging wheel decides when each step happens.

01 · Store the energy

The mainspring

A fully wound spring holds about forty hours of energy. So why not just let it turn the hands?

Turning the crown coils a long, thin steel ribbon inside a drum called the barrel. Wound tight around the centre, the spring wants to open out again. That push is the only power the watch ever gets.

Like a wind-up toy car. Wind it, put it down and let go. It races off at full speed and is done in seconds.

TryDrag the crown upward to wind the spring. Watch the coils pack in around the centre. Then press Let go.

Eureka 1

Wind it up, then let it go.

It ran for about a second, not forty hours. A spring is a store of energy, not a clock. It has power but no patience. Everything else in a watch exists to spend that energy slowly, in equal pieces.

02 · Divide it

The gear train

How can one spring turn one hand once a minute and another once an hour?

The barrel has teeth around its edge. They drive a chain of wheels. Each wheel turns a small gear called a pinion on the next one, so each wheel spins faster than the last. The centre wheel carries the minute hand. The fourth wheel carries the seconds hand. The escape wheel sits at the very end.

Like bicycle gears, used to count. A big wheel turning a small one makes the small one spin several times for each turn of the big one, always the same number.

TryDrag the big wheel with the seconds hand all the way round once. Keep an eye on the minute hand.

Eureka 2

Turn the seconds wheel once all the way round.

One turn of the seconds hand moved the minute hand exactly one minute. Gears count. They make sure 60 seconds always add up to one minute, and 60 minutes to one hour.

But notice who set the speed: you did. Spin faster and an hour flies by. The gears have no idea how long a second is.

03 · Let it out in steps

The escapement

So what decides how fast the wheels may turn?

At the end of the train sits the escape wheel, with fifteen hooked teeth. The spring is always pushing it round. A small rocking lever, the pallet fork, holds it with two red jewels called stones. Each time the fork rocks, one stone lifts off a tooth and the other drops in front of the next. The wheel jumps half a tooth, then stops. That is the tick.

Like a turnstile. The crowd pushes all the time, but only one person gets through per click. The turnstile, not the crowd, decides the pace.

TryYou are the watch's heartbeat now. Tap the drawing about once a second, as steadily as you can. The amber hand is your clock. The blue hand is real time.

Eureka 3

Keep ticking for about fifteen seconds and compare the two hands.

Your clock drifted away from real time.

The escapement turns the spring's constant push into steps you can count. But a watch is only as good as whatever rocks the fork. A human hand is not steady enough. Nobody's is.

04 · Keep the beat

The balance wheel

What could rock the fork perfectly evenly, all day long, as the spring weakens?

The balance wheel is a small ring mounted on a coiled hairspring thinner than a human hair. Twist it and let go. The hairspring pulls it back, it overshoots, and the hairspring pulls it the other way. Back and forth, back and forth.

Like a playground swing. Push it high or push it gently: each trip back and forth takes about the same time.

TryPress Release both. The left wheel starts with a huge swing, the right with a tiny one. Watch their orange tick lights. Then make the right hairspring shorter.

Eureka 4

Release both and count a few ticks.

The big swing and the small swing tick together. A big swing travels further, but it also moves faster, so every beat takes the same time. That is why a watch keeps time even as its spring runs down and the swings get smaller.

Change the hairspring and the beat changes. That is exactly how a watchmaker adjusts a watch: by shortening or lengthening the hairspring by a hair's width.

05 · The handshake

Who keeps pushing the swing?

A swing slowly loses height to friction and stops. So what keeps the balance going for two days?

The balance keeps good time but runs out of energy. The escape wheel has energy but no sense of time. So they trade. Each time the balance swings through the middle, a tiny jewel pin on it knocks the fork. The fork releases the escape wheel. As the tooth slides across the stone it pushes the fork, and the fork passes that push back to the balance. Then the other stone catches the wheel, and the balance swings on, free and untouched.

Like a parent pushing a child on a swing. One quick push as the swing passes the bottom, then hands off. The swing keeps its own rhythm. The push only keeps it going.

TryWatch one beat in slow motion, or press Step. Then press Disconnect the spring and watch the swing size.

Eureka 5

Disconnect the spring and watch the swing size.

Without the spring's pushes, the swings shrink until the pin can no longer knock the fork, and the watch stops. With them, the balance swings for as long as the spring lasts.

The escapement is the parent at the swing: a quick push at the bottom of every swing, hands off the rest of the time, so the balance keeps its own rhythm. It is also why a stopped watch needs a little shake after winding. The balance needs its first swing.

06 · Put it back together

The whole watch

So which part is really in charge: the strong spring or the tiny balance?

This is what you would see through the glass back of a watch. Polished silver bridges hold every wheel in place on red jewel bearings. Under them, the barrel pushes the wheels, the wheels push the escape wheel, and the fork lets it through half a tooth at a time, timed by the balance.

TryPress and hold the balance wheel. Then lift the bridges, trace the energy, and tap the parts to find each one.

Eureka 6

Press and hold the balance wheel.

Hold the smallest moving part and the whole watch freezes, right back to the spring. The spring has the power, but the balance has the say.

A watch is a spring that is only allowed to unwind one tick at a time, and a swinging wheel decides when each tick happens.

The whole idea

A spring, a swing and a handshake

Spring stores the energy → gears count it out → escapement releases it one tick at a time → balance decides when → and gets a small push back every swing.

Hold a watch to your ear. The ticking you hear is the handshake: the stones landing on the escape wheel, eight times every second, for as long as the spring lasts.

Notes on these drawings

The drawings are simplified so the moving parts stay readable. Tooth shapes, the shape of the stones, the springs and the timing of each release are schematic. The gears that turn the hour hand and the parts that let the crown set the time are left out. Swing sizes and the loss to friction in chapter 5 are exaggerated so the effect shows within a few beats.

The tooth counts (an 80-tooth barrel, a 15-tooth escape wheel, ratios of 8, 8, 7.5 and 16) and the 4 Hz balance are typical of a hand-wound watch, not a copy of any particular one. The motion is driven by a single clock and the ratios are exact: the seconds hand really does turn once for every 480 ticks.
